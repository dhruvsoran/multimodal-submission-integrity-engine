An electric vehicle charging network is an infrastructure system of publicly accessible charging stations deployed along roadways and in urban areas to support battery electric vehicles. Charging stations are typically categorised into three levels based on power output. Level 1 charging uses a standard household outlet and delivers the slowest charge. Level 2 charging uses dedicated equipment rated between 7 and 19 kilowatts and is the most common public option, while DC fast charging delivers 50 to 350 kilowatts and can recharge most vehicles in under an hour.

Network operators generally adopt one of two business models. In the asset-owner model, the operator finances, builds and maintains the stations and earns revenue from electricity sales and usage fees. In the franchise model, site hosts such as retailers or fuel stations own the hardware and pay the network operator for software, billing services and roaming access. Revenue per station depends heavily on utilisation, and industry analysis suggests that most public stations require utilisation above fifteen percent to reach breakeven.

Grid integration is a central planning concern. Unmanaged charging can coincide with evening demand peaks and force costly network upgrades, so utilities increasingly deploy smart-charging programmes that shift load to overnight periods or solar-rich hours. Vehicle-to-grid trials further allow parked vehicles to export electricity back to the grid during short-duration peaks, transforming commercial fleets into distributed storage assets.
