Congestion pricing is a system of surcharges levied on vehicles entering a designated urban zone during peak periods, intended to reduce traffic congestion and shift travel towards public transport. The economic rationale is that road space is underpriced, so drivers impose delay costs on others without compensating them; a charge internalises this externality.

Reported outcomes from operating schemes include double-digit reductions in vehicles entering the charged zone, measurable gains in bus speeds, and stable or increased public-transport ridership. Revenue is typically earmarked for transit improvements, which is considered essential for public acceptance. Equity concerns are prominent: charges are regressive for low-income drivers who cannot shift departure times, so schemes commonly pair exemptions, discounts or improved off-peak service for affected groups.

Political feasibility is widely regarded as the binding constraint. Public support tends to rise after implementation, once travel-time benefits become visible, but opponents before launch frequently frame the charge as an additional tax. Analysts therefore recommend early investment in alternatives such as park-and-ride facilities and bus priority corridors, transparent reporting of revenue use, and piloting with modest charge levels before full deployment.
