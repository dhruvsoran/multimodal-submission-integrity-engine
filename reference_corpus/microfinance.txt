Microfinance refers to small loans, savings products and insurance offered to low-income households and micro-enterprises that lack access to conventional banking. Group-lending models, in which borrowers guarantee one another and build credit history through small repayments, remain the dominant mechanism for first-time borrowers.

Evidence on impact is nuanced. Access to microloans supports business formation and income smoothing, but average returns vary widely, and over-indebtedness emerges where multiple lenders serve the same borrowers. Studies consistently find that repayment performance improves when underwriting uses cash-flow signals rather than collateral: utility and rent payment histories, mobile-money records and supplier invoices all predict repayment for applicants with thin credit files.

Digital delivery has transformed operating costs. Pre-filled applications drawn from administrative records shorten completion times, reduce abandonment, and allow lenders to approve marginal applicants safely. Consumer-protection rules matter: transparent pricing, capped rollovers, and accessible complaint channels are associated with lower default and higher customer retention. Partnerships between public funds and community banks extend reach, because community banks already maintain merchant relationships and can originate loans while the fund bears first-loss risk.
