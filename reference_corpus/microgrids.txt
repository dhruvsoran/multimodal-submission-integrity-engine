A microgrid is a localised grouping of electricity sources and loads that normally operates connected to a traditional wide-area synchronous grid, but can also disconnect and operate autonomously as economic or physical conditions require. Microgrids typically combine distributed generation such as rooftop solar photovoltaics, small wind turbines, and combined heat and power units with battery energy storage and intelligent switching hardware.

The economic case for a microgrid rests on three value streams: avoided peak demand charges, reduced transmission losses from on-site generation, and resilience benefits during grid outages. Studies of commercial microgrids report peak-load reductions between ten and thirty percent depending on the mix of storage and flexible loads. Battery capacity is usually the dominant capital cost, and oversized batteries spend most of the year idle, so designers commonly undersize storage and accept limited spillover during extreme events.

Governance is a recurring barrier. Microgrids serve multiple customers whose tariffs, reliability preferences and connection rights must be negotiated in advance. Regulatory frameworks in many jurisdictions still treat islanded systems as utilities, which complicates ownership and billing. Successful projects therefore secure anchor customers with predictable load profiles, such as industrial estates, campuses or healthcare facilities, before financing the remaining network.
